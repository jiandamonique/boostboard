What Mutual Aid Is

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-12

The term "mutual aid" gets used a lot right now, and not always with much precision. Here's a plain version: people meeting each other's needs directly, without waiting on a larger system to do it for them. No ideology required, no test to pass. A neighborhood cooking for a family after a house fire, coworkers pooling gas money for someone whose car broke down, a group chat that quietly covers a friend's rent for a month -- all of that is mutual aid. Most people have already taken part in some version of it, whether or not they ever called it that.

None of this is new. It's a name for something people have always done, long before the term became common -- the same instinct behind a barn-raising or a potluck after a funeral, communities pooling labor and food because that's simply what neighbors did for each other. What changed recently is visibility -- more of it started happening in public, online, where more people could see it.

Why the pandemic brought it back into view

During the pandemic, mutual aid networks became visible at a scale a lot of people hadn't seen before. Local groups organized grocery runs for people who couldn't leave home, medication pickups, small direct cash transfers -- fast, informal, and often faster than any official channel could move. It wasn't a new idea. It was an old idea, done at volume, at a moment when a lot of formal systems were too slow or too overwhelmed to keep up.

One book from that period is worth knowing about if the concept is new to you: Mutual Aid: Building Solidarity During This Crisis (and the Next) by Dean Spade, published in 2020. It's a short, practical explanation of the idea -- not a political manifesto, just a clear account of what mutual aid is, how it's organized, and why it works when it works. A second edition is available for preorder directly from the author, or check a local library or bookstore for the first edition.

The backlash, and where it comes from

Lately there's also been more pushback -- more suspicion of people who ask for help, more assumptions about why they're asking, more scrutiny of how the money gets used. Some of that comes from real, common-sense caution, and that's fair. But a lot of it seems to come from something else: a discomfort with the fact that people still need to ask at all.

Here's the thing worth sitting with: by and large, people don't ask if they don't have to. Asking publicly for help costs something -- pride, privacy, a kind of exposure most people would rather avoid. The person posting a fundraiser has usually already tried the quieter options first. Treating every request with suspicion by default gets the odds backwards.

Today's version of an old problem

It's a strange time to need public help. A request that should be a private, ordinary act -- ask, receive, move on -- instead gets put on display, judged, sometimes picked apart by strangers before anyone decides whether to help. It can feel less like asking a neighbor and more like stepping into an arena to be judged by a crowd, for a need that was never anyone else's business to evaluate in the first place.

That's a big part of why this project exists. Restoring some of that ordinary dignity -- the version where asking for help doesn't require winning over an audience first. A campaign shouldn't need to perform to be worth helping. It should be enough that someone needs it.

If any of this resonates, one of the simplest things you can do is look at a campaign that's still waiting for its first donation and decide for yourself whether it's worth a share or a few dollars -- no performance required on their end, and none on yours either.
