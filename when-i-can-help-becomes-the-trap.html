When "I Can Help" Becomes the Trap

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-29

When you launch a fundraiser, you are sharing something deeply personal. Whether you're trying to cover an unexpected medical bill, keep a roof over your head, or rally behind a loved one in crisis, putting a campaign into the world takes courage. It means standing up in public and saying: we can't do this alone.

That kind of openness should be met with community and care. A pattern has emerged that treats it as something else.

A familiar playbook

If you've ever looked for work while unemployed, the emotional mechanics will feel familiar.

Job seekers are regularly approached by accounts posing as recruiters. The message sounds full of promise: "Your background is incredible. I have an opening that fits you perfectly, but first you need our certified resume review," or "Pay a one-time fee to access our exclusive employer network." The scheme targets the person who can least afford it. Someone looking for a paycheck is asked to write one.

Campaign organizers face the same structure. The moment a fundraiser goes live, the inbox fills up — not with donors, but with people presenting themselves as "promoters," "fundraising consultants," and "donor network managers." They move quickly on the anxiety every organizer feels after launching: what if no one sees this?

What the approach looks like

It rarely announces itself as a problem. The first message sounds like genuine empathy:

"I came across your story and was so moved. I work with a group of philanthropists who love backing causes like yours."

"I know an account with 500,000 followers that can share this today. Let's talk in DMs." "I help families in your exact situation hit their goals every week."

The message moves the conversation off the public post and into a private channel. Once there, the structure becomes clearer — a small fee, then a service charge, then a payment to unlock a donor list. Each step is framed as an investment in your loved one's relief. By the time the pattern is obvious, you've parted with money you didn't have to lose, and continuing feels easier than walking away.

The people most likely to hear "I can help you raise money — for a fee" are the people raising money because they don't have enough.

What real support looks like

Real donors find the campaign link and give on the platform where the funds are held. Real advocates share the direct link with people they know, with a line about why it caught their attention. Genuine private support means someone reaching out to give, with no ask attached.

That's distinct from a real arrangement with someone you already know and trust — a friend, a family member, a professional you sought out yourself. If you've negotiated terms together and both feel the agreement is fair, that's a working relationship, and a worker is worth their wage. The difference tends to show up in how it started: a legitimate arrangement is mutual and unhurried, not cold, not private, and not structured around urgency and escalating payments.

When it comes from a stranger, moves fast, and asks you to pay before anything real has happened — that's the offer to step back from.
