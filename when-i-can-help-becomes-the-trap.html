When "I Can Help" Becomes the Trap

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-29

Real support for a fundraiser still looks more or less the same as it always has. Someone reads the campaign, finds a real connection to the story, and shares the original link with a person they know — with a sentence about why it caught their attention. No fee. No intermediary. Nothing to sign up for. The link goes directly to the platform that's holding the funds.

During the pandemic, that kind of support happened out in the open, visibly, in public threads. "Sharing this now." "Just sent something." You could see it happening, and so could the person who needed it.

That's less common now — but it's still real support, and it still works.

Some private help is genuine

Not all help that happens privately is suspicious. Someone sends a payment directly and quietly. A connection is made between two people who both prefer it that way. Someone reaches out privately because they don't want attention drawn to either side of the exchange. That's normal, and it's fine.

What's different is when the private channel isn't where the help happens — it's where the pitch starts.

The pattern worth knowing

It usually sounds warm and vague at first. "I know someone who can help you." "I've worked with people in your exact situation." "Let me connect you with the right people." The conversation moves off the public thread. Once it does, the structure becomes clearer: buy this service, pay for this package, email this person and say this, pay a small amount to get in front of the right audience.

It's priming the pump — creating just enough sense of access and momentum that each small step feels like progress toward something real. By the time the pattern is obvious, you've already invested. You're in a spam and scam funnel, and the path back out feels more costly than continuing.

The people most likely to hear "I can help you raise money — for a fee" are the people raising money because they don't have enough. The offer targets the exact situation it claims to solve.

This isn't limited to fundraising. Job seekers encounter the same structure from fake recruiters: a warm introduction, a vague promise of access, and then fees for resume services or platform access that lead nowhere. The emotional logic is identical — hope, a credible-sounding offer, and then a series of small payments that compound before anyone pauses to ask what they've bought.

What real help still looks like

The most useful thing anyone can do for a fundraiser is still free and still public. Read the original campaign. Share the direct link — not a referral link, not a repost through another platform, the original — with someone you know, and say why it caught your attention. That's it.

Boost Board exists to make that step a little easier. The campaigns here have been reviewed by a real person. The links go directly to the original fundraising platform. There's no DM required, no service to sign up for, no fee. If a campaign is on the board, the path to helping is the same one that was always there.

Public support is harder to fake than a private offer. That's not a drawback — that's how you know it's real.
