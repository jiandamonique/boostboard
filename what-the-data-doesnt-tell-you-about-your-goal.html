Why Your Goal Amount Matters. Give it Some Thought.

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-22

When you set up a crowdfunding campaign, most platforms will helpfully show you what similar campaigns raised. It's framed as guidance. Set your goal here, it suggests, because campaigns like yours tend to reach that range.

We've looked at a lot of these campaigns. That number deserves some scrutiny.

What "similar campaigns" actually includes

The average a platform shows you is typically drawn from a broad category — personal hardship, medical costs, community support — that includes everyone who filed under that label. The people at the top of that range are often there because of something the data doesn't capture: an existing audience they could share to, a moment that went viral, a community organization or employer who amplified their campaign, press coverage, or a network that was already primed to give.

None of that is wrong, exactly. It's just not your situation, necessarily. The average is real. It just might not be about you.

Your goal isn't only a number — it's a signal

Here's what we notice watching campaigns from the outside: the goal amount changes how a stranger reads the page before they've decided anything.

A $10,000 goal sitting at zero looks like a wall. Not an unworthy cause — just a long way from where it needs to go, and a first-time donor has no way to know whether their $25 will make a dent or just disappear into the distance. A $500 goal at zero looks like a door. The same $25 feels like it matters differently.

This isn't about lowballing what you need. Your actual need is your actual need. But your funding goal and your actual need don't have to be the same number — and a lot of experienced organizers already handle it that way without naming it as a strategy. They set a goal they believe they can reach with the network they actually have, and if they hit it, they extend. That approach has a name now on most platforms: it's called an extension or a stretch goal. It's not a workaround. It's how staged fundraising works.

What hitting a goal actually does for you

Reaching 100% — even on a smaller goal — changes the page. Donors feel like they completed something, which makes them more likely to share. Some platforms surface completed or nearly-complete campaigns differently. And practically speaking, it gives you something true and specific to say when you follow up: "We hit the first goal — here's what's next."

Stalling at 12% of a large goal for six weeks tells a different story, even if the dollar amount raised is identical. The percentage is what people see.

We're not telling you what your need should be

We want to be clear about that. We've seen campaigns that genuinely needed $15,000, asked for it, and were right to. The point isn't that small goals are better. The point is that the number the platform suggests is based on aggregate data that may have very little to do with your starting position — and it's worth thinking about what goal you can actually reach before you commit to one.

Ask yourself: who is in my network right now? What could I realistically raise from the people who already know me, before anyone new finds this page? That number is probably your first goal. Everything beyond it is what tools like Boost Board are here to help with.

We don't track or report individual campaign goals or outcomes. This post draws on patterns we observe across the campaigns submitted to Boost Board and general public information about how crowdfunding platforms present data to organizers.
