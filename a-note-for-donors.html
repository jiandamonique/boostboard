A Note for Donors: Why the Loudest Campaign Isn't the One That Needs You Most

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-07

Crowdfunding runs on the same basic engine as everything else online: whoever tells the story best gets seen the most. That's not a knock on any individual campaign — it's just how the system is built. A campaign with a sharp headline, a compelling photo, and an organizer with a big, generous network is going to outperform an equally real, equally urgent need described by someone without those advantages. Attention follows communication skill and social reach, not the size of the need behind it.

Multiple peer-reviewed studies on medical crowdfunding — including research published in the American Journal of Public Health — have found that roughly 88–90% of GoFundMe medical campaigns never reach their stated goal, and that a meaningful share receive no donations at all. The same body of research has found that campaign success tracks with an organizer's existing social and financial resources at least as much as it tracks with the size of their need. Sources: Kenworthy et al., American Journal of Public Health; related studies in Social Science & Medicine and PLOS ONE.

Put plainly: most people who ask for help on these platforms don't get the help they were asking for. That's not because the need wasn't real. It's because visibility on these platforms was never distributed evenly to begin with.

The tired feeling isn't wrong, either

If you've ever felt like every dollar you give is just circling back through the same small group of people you know — or worried that you're one bad week away from needing to ask the same thing of others — that instinct isn't paranoia. It's a reasonable read of a system where a small number of highly visible campaigns absorb a large share of total donations, while everyone else competes for what's left. Add a nonstop feed of bad news on top of that, and it's no surprise that a lot of people want to help and don't know where to start, or have simply run out of gas trying.

There's genuinely no single correct way to give. But a few honest questions can help you figure out where your specific time, money, or attention will do something — rather than just adding to the noise.

Questions worth asking yourself before you give

Emergency, or root cause?

Most giving happens in response to something sudden — a diagnosis, a disaster, a fire. That instinct is good. But ongoing support for something like a chronic illness, long-term recovery, or community infrastructure matters just as much, even though it never comes with the same urgency.

A person, or an organization?

Giving directly to someone can be the simplest, most immediate form of help there is — nothing about that is lesser. But an established organization may have the infrastructure to handle a complex or long-running problem more efficiently than any one campaign can.

Close to home, or further out?

Helping a neighbor or a local family creates a kind of connection that's hard to replace. It's also worth considering that the same dollar can go further somewhere with fewer existing resources — a different neighborhood, a different country entirely.

Money, or something else?

Donating is one lever, not the only one. Time, a specific skill, or simply sharing a campaign with people who haven't seen it yet are all real forms of help — and none of them require having spare cash on hand.

How do you check something is legitimate?

For organizations, tools like Charity Navigator or GiveWell exist specifically to help with this. For individual campaigns, platforms generally offer some baseline fraud protection, but the real work — cross-checking a story, watching for inconsistencies — is on you. A trusted local or mutual aid group can sometimes do that legwork for you.

Where Boost Board fits into this

It's one small, honest attempt at correcting for exactly the imbalance the research above describes.
