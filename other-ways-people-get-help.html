The Other Ways People Get Help

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-20

Crowdfunding is one path to support, and it's the one this board exists to help with -- but it's rarely the only one, and it doesn't work at the same speed for everyone. It's worth naming a few of the other real, practical channels people use, alongside crowdfunding rather than instead of it.

  • Local mutual aid networks — mutualaidhub.org maintains a directory searchable by location; many cities also have their own, and these tend to move faster than any national platform since they're run by people nearby.
  • Community fridges and pantries — for immediate food needs, often no application or waiting period involved.
  • Benevolence funds tied to a specific faith community or membership organization — many exist quietly, known mainly to their own members.
  • Patient-assistance programs for medical costs — often run directly by drug manufacturers, hospitals, or disease-specific nonprofits, and can cover costs a crowdfunding goal was never going to fully close anyway.
  • Employer emergency-relief funds, where they exist — some companies quietly maintain one for employees in crisis, separate from any HR process.

None of these compete with a crowdfunding campaign, and using one doesn't mean a campaign wasn't worth starting. They're just faster for some situations, and a crowdfunding campaign can keep running in parallel the whole time.

What moves people to give

Beyond sympathy for the situation, two things tend to matter more than people expect: specificity and agency. "$40 covers one vet visit" does more than a general appeal, because it turns a big, vague need into something a person can picture handing over themselves. And knowing exactly what a share does -- who it reaches, why it matters -- gives someone a concrete way to help even when they have no money to give.

Momentum matters more than most people think, too. A fundraiser sitting at $1 of a $1,600 goal can read as abandoned, even if it's brand new. One at $40 reads differently -- like someone already looked at it and believed it was worth something. That's part of why the first few dollars matter disproportionately, and why a single early donation or share can be worth more than its size suggests.

On waiting for other systems

A lot of the people on this board have already tried, or are still waiting on, some kind of formal assistance -- a government program, an insurance claim, a benefits application. Those processes are often slow, sometimes uncertain in their outcome, and can exclude people who don't meet a specific set of criteria even when the need is real. That's a legitimate frustration on its own, and it's a real part of why people turn to their own community for support instead: not because the formal system doesn't exist, but because it doesn't always arrive in time, or at all.

If you're weighing where to put your own time or money, all of these are worth knowing about -- and none of them make a crowdfunding campaign any less worth supporting.
