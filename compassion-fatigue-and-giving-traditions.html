Compassion Fatigue Is Real. We can only do what we can.

Volunteer Team · 2026-09-11

Every day, this board asks you to consider someone new. That's the whole idea — a fresh campaign, a fresh chance for it to be seen. But we'd be doing something dishonest if we never acknowledged the obvious tension in that: asking for anything, every day, from the same people, eventually runs into a real limit. It has a name, and it isn't a personal failing.

This is a documented thing, not just a feeling

"Donor fatigue" is real enough to have its own dictionary entry — Merriam-Webster defines a sense of fatigue as "a state or attitude of indifference or apathy brought on by overexposure," using donor fatigue itself as the example, and cross-referencing the related, more clinical term "compassion fatigue." It isn't apathy toward the cause. It's what tends to happen to anyone, however much they care, once the asking has no natural edges.

A tradition built with an edge already in it

Tzedakah, the Jewish practice of giving, is a useful example specifically because it was never left open-ended in the first place. The word comes from tzedek — justice — and tzedakah is framed as a mitzvah, an obligation toward justice, rather than an act of pity or a spontaneous impulse. Maimonides wrote that giving roughly a tenth of one's income is the standard, sound way to fulfill it.

What's easy to miss is that the tradition also names an upper limit. Maimonides warned that giving more than about a fifth is "foolhardy" outside genuine emergencies, and that no one should give so much that they put themselves at risk of needing to receive tzedakah from someone else. The boundary isn't a loophole around generosity. It's what was understood, a long time ago, to make generosity last.

Other traditions carry a version of the same idea — zakat in Islam, tithing in various Christian traditions, dana in Buddhist practice — each with its own specifics we won't try to summarize here, but each treating giving as something structured and bounded, not unlimited. Plenty of people arrive at something similar without any religious framing at all: a set monthly amount, a donor-advised fund, a personal rule about which causes get a yes. The structure is the point, whatever shape it takes.

Sharing still counts, always

This is the other reason we keep coming back to it: sharing a campaign costs nothing and genuinely helps, based on actual research, not just something we tell people to make them feel better. If money is tapped out but you still want to help, sharing is not a lesser option. It's a different one, and it's real.

What we'd want for you

Set whatever limit makes sense for your life — a dollar amount, a monthly cadence, a rule about only ever giving to one thing at a time. Then give within it and let that be enough, guilt-free, even on the days when the board features someone whose story genuinely moves you and the answer still has to be no. That's not failing the mission. It's the only way anyone keeps showing up to it long-term — the same conclusion a lot of very old traditions already reached.
